Section 1: People claiming asylum in the UK, 2010 to 2025

One thing worth flagging, not an error but a definitional wrinkle. When the year-ending-December-2024 statistics were first published (Feb 2025), the Home Office headline "people claiming asylum" figure for 2024 was 108,138. The 104,764 you're using reflects a later, revised vintage (which is what the Migration Observatory and the May 2026 Commons Library briefing now use), so it's the more current number — just be aware some sources still cite 108,138 for 2024. If your whole series is drawn from one consistent vintage (it appears to be), you're fine; just don't mix it with the 108,138 figure in the same chart.

The label "People claiming asylum" is the correct term for this series (it includes dependants), so that's accurate as written.


Section 2: Initial decisions by outcome, year ending March 2026

Problems
A withdrawal is not an initial decision.
The 128,300 initial decisions comprise grants and refusals. Withdrawals are non-substantive outcomes outside that total. Therefore, “Initial decisions by outcome” is misleading when the chart includes withdrawals.

“16,901 claims were withdrawn” has the wrong unit.
The figure counts people, including dependants—not distinct claims or main-applicant cases. The Home Office’s wording is: 16,901 people had their asylum claims withdrawn.

The chart is not a complete presentation of all outcomes.
The detailed table also records 5,931 administrative outcomes during the period. The three displayed categories therefore must not be presented as an exhaustive breakdown of “initial outcomes.” Detailed Home Office dataset, Asy_D02.

Recommended wording
Title
Initial decisions and withdrawals, year ending March 2026

Summary
Of 128,300 people receiving an initial decision, 48,581 were granted protection or other leave and 79,719 were refused. Separately, 16,901 people had their asylum claims withdrawn.

Note
The existing note is accurate. A slightly clearer version would be:
The published grant rate of 39% is calculated from main-applicant cases. On the people basis shown here, 48,581 ÷ 128,300 gives 37.9%; this is a different measure and illustrates why the basis must be checked before dividing one figure by another.

The Home Office marks data from 2025 Q1 onward as provisional. No document edits have been made.

Rounding clarification
The displayed immigration and emigration changes imply a net-migration fall of approximately 516,000:
£-429,000 - 87,000 = -516,000

The published net-migration series falls by 517,000. This is not an error: ONS calculates net migration from unrounded estimates and then rounds each published figure independently.
Recommended summary
The existing summary is accurate. For slightly greater precision:
Immigration was highest in 2023 on this December-ending calendar-year series, at 1,441,000, and fell in both subsequent years. Emigration increased each year from 480,000 in 2021 to 680,000 in 2024, before easing to a provisional 642,000 in 2025.

Recommended note
Between 2023 and 2024, calendar-year net migration fell by around 517,000. Immigration fell by around 429,000 while emigration rose by roughly 87,000: more people leaving contributed to the fall, alongside fewer people arriving. The components may not reconcile exactly after rounding because ONS calculates from unrounded estimates. The highest rolling 12-month immigration estimate was 1,469,000 in the year ending March 2023.

Part 2.2 — “Immigrants are a drain on the public finances”
Verdict: substantial revision required. The conclusion that this sweeping claim is not established is defensible, but the page currently mischaracterises the supporting research.
Central statistical problem
The Migration Observatory says that pre-Brexit static studies produced fiscal effects smaller than 1% of GDP in magnitude. It does not present “plus or minus 1%” as an uncertainty interval around one estimate.
The page therefore should not say:
“That range spans zero.”

The studies cover different periods, populations and definitions. Combining their results into a range and treating that range as evidence that one common estimate crosses zero is not statistically valid.
Furthermore, the source expressly says that no fiscal analysis covers the entire post-Brexit immigration system. The article’s “Across academic studies” wording is consequently too broad. Migration Observatory fiscal-impact briefing
Recommended short answer
Not established as a general claim. Pre-Brexit studies of the migrant population found fiscal effects smaller than 1% of GDP in magnitude, but their results vary with the period, migrant group, comparison and method. No equivalent analysis covers the whole post-Brexit immigration system.

This also fixes the comma splice in the existing answer.
Other corrections
Replace “Across academic studies” with “Across the pre-Brexit static studies summarised by the Migration Observatory.”
Delete “That range spans zero” and the claim that the evidence is necessarily consistent with both signs.
Replace “changing any one of them moves the result across zero” with “changing these assumptions can materially affect the result and, in some comparisons, its sign.”
Define what “drain” means: an absolute negative fiscal balance, or a less favourable balance than the UK-born population. These are different tests, especially when the whole country is running a deficit.
Change the period metadata from “studies reviewed to October 2024.” The cited briefing was published in June 2026, while its complete-population estimates concern pre-Brexit periods.
OBR illustration
The £341,000 figure is broadly verified against the OBR’s corrected chart, but the description omits essential conditions. It represents an illustrative average-wage migrant worker arriving aged 25 and living to 80, with no dependants—not an average observed migrant outcome.
Recommended wording:
“In one OBR illustration, an average-wage migrant worker arriving aged 25, with no dependants and remaining until age 80, has a cumulative net fiscal contribution of about £341,000.”

The OBR stresses that earnings, age, dependants and length of stay can change the result substantially. OBR fiscal-risks report and corrected chart
Asylum expenditure
The £4.9 billion figure is correct for estimated Home Office and Ministry of Justice asylum expenditure in 2024–25. It excludes some expenditure, particularly local-authority costs.
However, remove “and rising.” The cited evidence establishes the level but does not establish that every relevant measure of asylum spending was rising; direct asylum-support expenditure actually fell from approximately £4.7 billion to £4 billion. Also describe £4.9 billion as an NAO estimate based on actual costs, rather than simply “audited.” https://www.nao.org.uk/press-releases/four-critical-requirements-needed-for-a-fair-efficient-asylum-system/
