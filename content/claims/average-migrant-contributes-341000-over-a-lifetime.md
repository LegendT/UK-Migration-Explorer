## What the data says

**The Office for Budget Responsibility does not publish this number, or any number like it.**
What it published is a chart, 4.13 in *Fiscal risks and sustainability*, corrected in November
2024. The chart draws the cumulative net fiscal contribution of a representative migrant who
arrives at 25 on the UK average wage, modelled as an individual with no dependants, against a UK
resident on the same basis. It is a line running from age 25 to age 100. **OBR states no single
value anywhere in the text and singles out no age on the line.**

**Any figure quoted from it is somebody's choice of where to stop.** Read from the chart's own
data, the line rises through working life, peaks while the person is in their sixties, falls once
they are drawing a pension and using health care, and is negative by age 100 where the chart ends.
Those readings are recorded in this site's own record for the figure, with the value at each age,
because the age moves the sign as well as the size. So the same chart supports a large positive
figure, a smaller positive figure, or a negative one, depending only on the age chosen, and
nothing in the claim says which age it used.

**This site reads it at 82 and publishes
£{{fiscal/illustrative-lifetime-net-fiscal-contribution-of-a-migrant}}.** That age is not a
better answer, it is a stated one: it is the life expectancy OBR's own text gives an average
person in 2028, which makes it the only candidate age the publication itself gives a reason for.
The reading is graded as an estimate rather than an official figure, because it is this site's
reading of a chart and not a value OBR prints.

**And this site published £341,000 until 3 August 2026.** That was the same chart read at 80, an
age nobody here had written down. It moved when the age was chosen and recorded, and no publisher
released anything new in between. That is the clearest available demonstration of what the claim
is doing: the figure changed by tens of thousands of pounds because somebody picked a different
year to stop at, and the page carrying it had looked equally confident before and after.

## Why the claim is misleading

**"The average migrant" is the wrong noun for a single modelled individual.** The chart's subject
is one hypothetical person with fixed characteristics: arriving at 25, earning the average wage,
with no dependants in the model. Real arrivals come at every age, on every wage, and many bring
dependants, whom the modelled individual does not have. An illustration built to show how the answer moves with
circumstances is being quoted as though it had averaged over them.

**The endpoint does the arithmetic, and it is never stated.** A claim of this shape is only
meaningful with an age attached, and the version that circulates carries none. Two people quoting
the same chart in good faith can differ by hundreds of thousands of pounds and neither is
misreading it.

**The same analysis makes a low-earning migrant a net cost.** Chart 4.13 draws the average-wage
case alongside a low-earnings one, which is negative over a lifetime, and a high-earnings one
considerably further positive. This site's page on
[whether immigrants pay more than they cost](/common-claims/immigrants-pay-far-more-than-they-cost/)
has said so since 27 July 2026. Quoting the average-wage line alone is selecting the middle of a
set whose spread is the finding.

**It presents a projection as a measurement.** The chart is a model of the future under stated
assumptions about earnings, taxes, benefits, health spending and pensions over seventy-five years.
Assumptions made in 2024 about 2099 are not observations, and OBR publishes them as risks and
scenarios for that reason.

**And the broader evidence does not settle in either direction.** Studies of the
[net fiscal impact](/what-the-words-mean/#fiscal-impact) of immigration find effects small in
magnitude relative to the economy, positive in some and negative in others, across different
periods, groups and methods. That is a set of separate studies rather than one estimate with a
margin around it, so it cannot be collapsed into a figure either. This site's page on
[whether immigrants pay more than they cost](/common-claims/immigrants-pay-far-more-than-they-cost/)
sets that out, and a single lifetime figure is not a shortcut past it.

## What the claim gets right

**The chart is real, it is official, and it is not a fringe source.** OBR is the government's own
fiscal watchdog and *Fiscal risks and sustainability* is one of its principal publications.
Somebody quoting a figure from chart 4.13 is reading a genuine document, and the correction here
is about what the chart can carry, not about whether it exists.

**On OBR's own central case the line does end positive within a normal lifetime.** At the ages a
person is likely to reach, the average-wage migrant arriving at 25 is a net contributor in the
model. That is why the reading published on this site is a positive number. The claim's direction
is defensible on that scenario; its precision and its noun are not.

**And migrants do pay charges that nobody disputes.** In the financial year 2025-26 the
immigration health surcharge raised about £{{fiscal/immigration-health-surcharge-revenue}}
billion and Home Office visa and immigration fee income about
£{{fiscal/home-office-visa-and-immigration-fee-income}} billion, both of which are collected
before any tax is paid and are counted in nobody's argument about whether the figure above is
341 or something else. Those are measurements rather than projections, and they are a single
year rather than the lifetime the claim is about.

## The mirror claim fails too

"Migrants cost the taxpayer over their lifetime" fails on exactly the same chart, and usually by
reading it at 100. That endpoint is as arbitrary as any other, and it is the one that produces the
most negative number available. Both versions work the same way: take a line whose whole purpose
is to show sensitivity, stop it at the age that suits, and quote the height as a fact.

## Better questions to ask

- At what age does the figure stop counting? Without that, the number means nothing.
- Whose figure is it? OBR publishes the chart and prints no value from it.
- Which migrant? The line is one modelled person on the average wage with no dependants.
- What happens to a low earner, or someone arriving at 45? Both are in the same analysis and both
  move the answer a long way.
- Is it a projection or a measurement? Seventy-five years of assumptions is the former.

## Source notes

The chart: Office for Budget Responsibility, *Fiscal risks and sustainability*, September 2024,
chart 4.13, corrected 14 November 2024. The figure this site publishes is read from the chart's
own data at age 82 and rounded to the nearest thousand. OBR prints no value from it, which is why
the record is graded as an estimate and says so.

The age: OBR's text gives 82 as the life expectancy of an average person in 2028. The choice was
made on 3 August 2026 and recorded in the record's own notes, along with the value the same line
gives at its peak in the person's sixties and at age 100 where the chart ends, because the age
moves the sign as well as the size. **Those two readings are the source for every statement above
about the shape of the line**, and they are in the data layer rather than on this page because
nobody publishes them: they are readings, like the one this site publishes, and putting them in
prose would give three unpublished numbers the look of three published ones.

The low-earnings and high-earnings cases: the same chart and the same record note. This site has
carried them since 27 July 2026 on the page linked above.

Fiscal impact studies: Migration Observatory, *The Fiscal Impact of Immigration in the UK*. The
range is across separate studies of different periods, groups and methods, and is not an
uncertainty interval around one estimate.

Health surcharge and fee income: Home Office annual report and accounts. Both are receipts rather
than modelled projections.

**This page does not offer a better single figure.** It has one because the data layer needs a
value to cite, and it says which age produced it. A reader who wants one number for the lifetime
contribution of the average migrant is asking for something no publisher provides.
