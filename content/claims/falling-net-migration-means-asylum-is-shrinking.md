## What the data says

Net migration has fallen a long way. It stands at {{migration/net-migration}} in the year
ending December 2025, against {{migration/net-migration-2}} the year before, and a peak of
{{migration/net-migration-peak}} in the year ending March 2023. The peak is a different
twelve-month window from the latest figure, so the gap between them is not a like-for-like
change, but the direction is not in doubt.

Asylum did not fall with it. {{asylum/asylum-applications-2025}} people claimed asylum in
calendar year 2025, the second highest figure in the calendar-year series this site holds,
behind 2024 alone. On the year ending March 2026, the measure the rest of this site uses,
the figure is {{asylum/asylum-applications}}.

So over the period in which net migration fell by most of its value, asylum claims stayed
near their record. The two series are not tracking each other, and one of them cannot be
read off the other.

## Why the claim is misleading

**They measure different things.** Net migration is a *net* figure: long-term arrivals minus
long-term departures, across every nationality and every reason for coming, British citizens
included. Asylum applications are a *gross* count of people who made a claim. A fall in net
migration can be produced entirely by more people leaving, which tells you nothing about how
many arrived, let alone how many claimed asylum.

**Asylum is a small part of the immigration side.** Asylum applicants were
{{migration/asylum-related-share-of-all-immigration}}% of all long-term immigration in the
year ending December 2025. A measure that small cannot drive the headline, and the headline
moving does not tell you what that measure did. The arithmetic runs in both directions: the
whole asylum figure could double and net migration would still be dominated by study, work
and departures.

**They come from different publishers on different bases.** Net migration is an ONS estimate,
an official statistic in development and provisional. Asylum applications are a Home Office
count from operational data. Neither is a version of the other, and a change in one is not
evidence about the other.

## What the claim gets right, and what actually shrank

Something in the asylum system did get smaller, and it is worth naming rather than glossing.
The queue of people waiting for a first decision stands at
{{asylum/asylum-backlog-people-awaiting-an-initial-decision}}, and the chart on the
[asylum page](/asylum/) shows it well below where it stood in 2022.

That fall is real, and it is not what the claim describes. It happened while claims stayed
near their record, so arithmetically it came from decisions being issued rather than from
fewer people claiming. Why decisions were issued faster is a question about casework this
site's figures cannot answer. And it did not shrink the system, it moved the work:
the appeals queue stood at {{asylum/asylum-appeals-backlog}} at 31 March 2026, against
{{asylum/asylum-appeals-backlog-previous-year}} a year earlier. Deciding a claim faster
produces a refusal that can be appealed, and the appeal is a queue of its own.

**So the claim reaches a partly correct conclusion by invalid reasoning**, which is the case
this site finds hardest to write about and the one most worth writing. One queue shrank. The
system did not, and net migration is not why.

## The mirror claim fails too

"Rising net migration means the asylum system is under more pressure" is wrong for the same
reason and in the same direction. Both treat one series as a proxy for another because the
two are talked about in the same breath. Neither would survive its author being asked which
denominator they meant.

## Better questions to ask

- Which asylum measure is meant: claims made, people waiting for a first decision, appeals
  outstanding, or people in supported accommodation? They move independently.
- Is the migration figure net or gross? A net fall can come entirely from departures.
- Over what period, and does the asylum figure cover the same twelve months?

## Source notes

Net migration: Office for National Statistics, long-term international migration,
provisional, year ending December 2025, published 21 May 2026. An official statistic in
development. ONS advises that estimates either side of June 2021 are not directly comparable,
because the method used to produce them changed.

Asylum applications and the initial-decision queue: Home Office, immigration system
statistics, year ending March 2026, published 21 May 2026, on the
[people basis](/what-the-words-mean/#people-and-cases), main applicants plus dependants.

Appeals: HM Courts and Tribunals Service / Ministry of Justice tribunals statistics. A
different publisher on a different cycle, and provisional.

**This page compares periods that do not line up, and says so rather than hiding it.** The
net migration peak is a year ending March; the latest net migration figure is a year ending
December; the asylum applications figure is a calendar year; the queues are point-in-time
stocks at 31 March 2026. None of those can be subtracted from another. The argument here does
not need them to be: it needs only that one series fell steeply while the other did not, which
holds on any of the periods available.

**On selection.** This site checks propositions that circulate rather than quotations
